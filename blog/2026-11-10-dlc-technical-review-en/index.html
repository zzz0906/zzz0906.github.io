    Training–Inference Mismatch in LLM RL: What DLC Corrects

    Review date: 2026-11-10 (UTC)
    Review author: Zhongzhu Zhou
    Paper reviewed: On the Origin and Evolution of Training-Inference Mismatch in LLM Reinforcement Learning
    Paper authors: Yu Li, Yufa Zhou, Tian Lan
    arXiv: 2610.40945v1
    Paper version: v1, 29 October 2026

    1. What this paper contributes

    A rollout worker can produce data that is off-policy for two quite different reasons. It can compute a different next-token distribution from the trainer even when both start from the same parameters. Or it can still be running yesterday’s policy in the much shorter sense of several optimizer steps ago. Both appear in the same logged ratio. They call for different repairs because only the second is directly amplified by the trainer’s own movement within a synchronization interval.

    The paper places a third distribution between the rollout and training distributions: run the trainer with the sampler’s weights. The three log-probabilities form an exact decomposition at a common prefix. The measurements then ask what happens after subtracting the off-policy mean score. Under the controlled numerical mismatch, the residual appears small compared with gradient noise. Under a frozen sampler, a residual that grows with the policy displacement feeds into the next update. The proposed discrepancy-lag correction (DLC) reweights and clips the version-dependent factor, subtracts the mean of that clipped score, and refreshes the sampler when a ratio moment signals loss of overlap.

    I find the diagnostic separation more broadly useful than the suggested threshold. The algebra does not depend on model size, but the reported one-half crossing is evidence from a particular training regime. In the main experiments DLC mostly buys fidelity to an on-policy run; in the paper’s timing table it pays for that fidelity with an extra forward and more expensive rollout logging. These are separate outcomes.

    2. Prerequisites: the data, the score, and the denominator

    2.1 Autoregressive actions and group rewards

    Given a prompt xxx, write ht=(x,y<t)h_t=(x,y_{<t})ht​=(x,y<t​). Each generated token is an action, with EOS or a length cap terminating the trajectory. A policy assigns the response a probability by multiplying its conditional probabilities. Taking logs turns products into sums, which makes per-token evidence easy to collect but does not make the sampled prefixes independent. Early actions determine which later states exist at all. A fix to the distribution of the current token cannot retroactively cause the sampler to visit a different prefix.

    In the main setup, eight completions of a prompt receive Countdown rewards of +1+1+1 or −1-1−1. The advantage of a completion is its reward minus the group mean, shared across tokens, without standard-deviation normalization. For example, three correct and five incorrect responses give a group mean of −0.25-0.25−0.25 and advantages +1.25+1.25+1.25 and −0.75-0.75−0.75. They sum to zero across responses. Once we condition on a prefix that occurs disproportionately in the correct completions, the mean need not be zero. In addition, token averaging weights long and short responses differently from response averaging. It matters which averaging operation a claim about zero mean refers to.

    2.2 Why the expected score is zero

    Ep[s]=∑vpv ∇θlog⁡pv=∑v∇θpv=∇θ1=0.\mathbb E_p[s]=\sum_v p_v\,\nabla_\theta\log p_v =\sum_v \nabla_\theta p_v =\nabla_\theta 1=0.Ep​[s]=v∑​pv​∇θ​logpv​=v∑​∇θ​pv​=∇θ​1=0.

    ∇θDKL(q∥pθ)=∇θ∑vqv(log⁡qv−log⁡pv)=−Eq[s].\nabla_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p_\theta) =\nabla_\theta\sum_v q_v(\log q_v-\log p_v) =-\mathbb E_q[s].∇θ​DKL​(q∥pθ​)=∇θ​v∑​qv​(logqv​−logpv​)=−Eq​[s].

    Add and subtract the product of means in the sampled update:

    Eq[as]=Eq[a]Eq[s]+Cov⁡q(a,s).\mathbb E_q[a s]=\mathbb E_q[a]\mathbb E_q[s]+\operatorname{Cov}_q(a,s).Eq​[as]=Eq​[a]Eq​[s]+Covq​(a,s).

    The first term is the paper’s drift. In gradient ascent, positive mean advantage pulls the current distribution toward the sampler on that prefix, while negative mean advantage pushes it away. It is possible to get both effects within one prompt even when the prompt-level rewards have been centered. The covariance term is what a change in relative advantages can teach the policy about alternative actions. That term still depends on which measure is used.

    2.3 What importance weighting buys

    At a fixed prefix and for a fixed advantage function with ppp supported by qqq, summing qv(pv/qv)avsvq_v(p_v/q_v)a_vs_vqv​(pv​/qv​)av​sv​ gives the ppp expectation. With a cap at 2, the difference is explicit:

    Eq[min⁡(p/q,2)as]−Ep[as]=−Eq[(p/q−2)+ as].\mathbb E_q[\min(p/q,2)a s]-\mathbb E_p[a s] =-\mathbb E_q[(p/q-2)_+\,a s].Eq​[min(p/q,2)as]−Ep​[as]=−Eq​[(p/q−2)+​as].

    The cap trades bias for protection from a small number of very large weights. A token with qv=0q_v=0qv​=0 cannot be recovered by changing a weight on observed tokens. A full-response likelihood ratio could in principle also correct where the prefixes and continuations come from, with support and the same definition of the return. The product across a long response can concentrate the weight on a tiny number of trajectories. It can be less usable than a biased local estimator combined with periodically refreshing the data.

    2.4 First fix what the probabilities mean

    Paper Table 1 is an engineering prerequisite for the analysis. If the sampler reports raw softmax probabilities after drawing from a top-p truncated distribution, the logged denominator is already wrong. If the trainer retokenizes decoded strings, the purported same action and prefix may differ. Reward-conditioned filtering and delayed long responses can change what distribution arrives in the training batch. The experiment uses temperature one, no truncation, shared token IDs and controlled batching. A system with those mismatches still present can compute a numerical difference of logs, but attributing it to precision and version alone would be wrong.

    An operator should preserve the actual draw-time version and report the probability of the actual sampling rule. Save positions and completion masks alongside token IDs, and separate the probability of admitting a trajectory from the probability of drawing its tokens. Logging one checkpoint ID per batch is insufficient when a worker can refresh midway through a response. In that case the version belongs to each action, and the prefix is a product under multiple generating policies.

    Thus the mean lag can be small even when individual log-ratios have already spread. The two offsets can have similar magnitudes at a particular step but enter the gradients differently.

    Algorithm 1. Collect a matched comparison

    1. Pin the generating version for each logged rollout token.
2. Save its prefix, token ID, sampled log q and top-H log q.
3. Replay the same token positions in trainer arithmetic at that version.
4. Replay the same positions at current weights; retain their scores.
5. Store e = log r - log q, lag = log p - log r and their sum.
6. Aggregate by version, sequence length and advantage, keeping tails.

    Use the sampler’s head HHH when asking for the reference probabilities; the reference model’s own top tokens can differ. A matched replay on shared checkpoints is stronger evidence for attribution than comparing one trained quantized run to an unrelated trained stale run. It also lets a researcher examine the same token’s ratio before and after a weight refresh, where comparing averages on different prefixes might hide a changing tail.

    6. Building the DLC update

    6.1 Clip the lag, not the combined discrepancy

    The denominator is the design choice. A weight p/qp/qp/q contains both factors; truncating it can cut a token purely because of numerical error. A PPO ratio with a before-batch reference is exactly one at the first update of a batch even when the data were sampled many versions ago. Subsequent inner updates bound movement relative to the before-batch policy, not relative to the generating version. DLC uses p/rp/rp/r and a sign-dependent PPO gradient mask:

    mv={1{ρv≤1+ε},At>0,1{1−ε≤ρv≤c},At<0.m_v= \begin{cases} \mathbf 1\{\rho_v\le 1+\varepsilon\}, & A_t>0,\\ \mathbf 1\{1-\varepsilon\le\rho_v\le c\}, & A_t<0 . \end{cases}mv​={1{ρv​≤1+ε},1{1−ε≤ρv​≤c},​At​>0,At​<0.​

    For At=0A_t=0At​=0 the term contributes zero. Paper values are ε=0.2\varepsilon=0.2ε=0.2, c=3c=3c=3.     7. Experiments: fidelity, reward, and time

    7.1 What is controlled

    The authors train non-thinking Qwen3-0.6B and 1.7B on Countdown for 40 steps with three runs per main configuration, 64 prompts and eight completions per prompt, 135 prompt and 377 response tokens as the caps. Rewards are binary signed values, with group mean subtraction and token averaging. Trainer compute is BF16 with FP32 master weights and FSDP2; SGD uses learning rate 0.01 without momentum, warmup or weight decay. Selected loop probes use 0.005. The reference uses vLLM BF16 and CUDA graphs. The quantized sampler uses FP8 block weights, dynamic activation quantization and FP8 KV cache; the frozen sampler has a nominal 64-step refresh interval so the unrefreshed 40-step run never gets a scheduled copy.

    The authors average full-vocabulary next-token KL from each trained policy to its paired on-policy policy over fixed prefixes generated by the initial checkpoint. The denominator is the mean KL between independently trained on-policy policies. A normalized deviation of one is a calibration point, not a confidence interval, and a value below one does not mean the policies are identical. Accuracy measures the trained model’s own responses on held-out Countdown; the paper evaluates eight responses per prompt on 64 problems. Keep the held-out return, fixed-prefix divergence, and direct runtime cost distinct.

    7.3 Refresh ablation, blindness of the fixed probe

    For 1.7B with frozen rollout, Table 4 gives DLC 0.6 times the floor and 51.9% at step 40, versus on-policy 57.6%. The three DLC runs refreshed once each (steps 20, 35 and 36). Importance sampling alone reached 115 times the floor and 0.5%; adding the same refresh trigger brought these to 2.6 times and 36.6%, with 2, 4 and 5 copies. The refresh alone therefore accounts for a large part of the recovery. The combination in DLC uses fewer copies in this study and gives better accuracy, but the experiment changes both the estimator and its subsequent refresh schedule.

    The 8B frozen test in Appendix C has one run per row. DLC has 3.1 times the floor and 49.6%, while IS with centering has 2.9 and 51.8%; on policy reaches 55.3%. In continuous asynchrony at 1.7B, the maximum gap is four versions and the token-weighted average is 2.85 versions. One run each yields 1.2 times the floor and 55.9% for DLC, 0.8 and 55.3% for admission DLC, 1.4 and 52.9% for IS, and 56.8% for on policy.     In the stale experiment DLC costs 113.2 seconds per step versus 50.4 on policy and 52.7 for IS with refresh. The reference forward accounts for 54.2 seconds in DLC, and generation 13.7 versus 6.5 seconds on policy. The authors identify about 19 seconds of redundant reference work on an unused KL path.     8. Limitations

    Coverage. Most evidence comes from one arithmetic task with short capped responses, a narrow family of dense models, a short training horizon and simple SGD. With Adam or Muon, the residual can modify optimizer state and change future steps even after a weight copy. MoE routing changes, stochastic kernels and rapidly drifting precision error can invalidate the empirical stationarity observed for the FP8 configuration. Longer responses increase the opportunity for prefix mismatch and can change which samples dominate token-level summaries.

    Precision of the bias diagnosis. The first-order comparison freezes parts of the dynamics and ignores continuation in the predicted bias. Table 10 shows that magnitude prediction remains weak even with exact likelihood terms. Similarly, the Gaussian clipping probabilities in appendix I are marginal ratio probabilities. Turning those into dropped shares by sign requires the sign-conditioned ratio distribution or a suitable independence assumption. Paper Figure 3 itself shows that advantage and lag can be associated. These expressions are useful references for diagnosing tails, not calibrated estimates of how much useful gradient the mask drops.

    Reference assumptions. ESS is a marginal overlap statistic, and the paper’s boundary at one half is calibrated on a small set of runs. The approximation for the tail of the clipped mean can fail in low-probability regions. In a mixed-version rollout, normalization for a complete prefix must account for the actual per-token version and preceding actions. Deferring high-lag responses is a data-selection operation: if rejected responses cluster by length, task, or reward, the distribution admitted for training differs from the rollout law.

    Evidence about cost and robustness. The large-model and continuously asynchronous rows are exploratory single runs. The paper times steps in its own configuration and does not hold throughput, wall clock, data volume, or quality target fixed across all comparisons. There is no evidence here that one method wins on elapsed time to the same accuracy. The rare-token share was evaluated on a limited number of checkpoints and uses a probability threshold tied to the initial policy. Legitimate exploration can also move into regions that initially had little probability.

    9. Critical analysis

    9.1 Use a two-view gate with a cost budget

    I would treat lag ESS as a warning on the worker’s data, and add a small, independent stream from the current trainer. Estimate the reciprocal moment and Kish fraction on matched tokens, stratified by generating version, response position, prompt class and advantage sign. Bootstrap by response, or by prompt group when using shared group rewards, so a cluster of long completions cannot masquerade as precise evidence. Log the fraction of gradient-bearing tokens dropped by the clip and the sum of their weighted advantages. This makes a case in which ESS is high but the few rare positive trajectories are clipped visible to the operator.

    For the second view, periodically sample the trainer on a held-out prompt pool and report task success, length, actual mass at low initial probability, and raw sequence KL before taking a ratio. As an illustration, a prefix that occurs with probability 10−510^{-5}10−5 under the old policy and 10−210^{-2}10−2 under the current policy would be expected 0.01 versus 10 times in 1000 trajectories. The old probe could miss it while it is routine enough to affect the trainer’s answers. A KL close to the on-policy floor on old prefixes is compatible with this example. Conversely, a rare-token warning without degrading task success could be benign exploration. A two-view gate can refresh or pause data admission when these signals agree and ask for a small fresh probe when they diverge.

    9.2 Separate a better estimator from better refresh timing

    Use a crossed experiment with the same prompt and seed streams: full DLC, lag weighting without centering, centering without lag weighting, and truncated combined weighting. For each estimator compare frozen, fixed refresh, and adaptive refresh, and add a matched-copy-budget schedule derived on held-out tuning runs. Fixed schedules reveal what the estimator buys for an identical data age cap; matched adaptive schedules reveal how often each estimator has to pay for a new version. Report all seeds, the full cost of reference replays, and paired uncertainty at a fixed compute budget. Continue past the first refresh so the regime includes multiple cycles.

    In the mixed-version case, randomize a fraction of borderline admission decisions for measurement, and record which prompts and lengths are deferred. This gives a way to assess selection bias and whether restarts disproportionately lose slow, difficult responses. For training, make the cost function include wasted rollout tokens and the waiting time of admitted samples. A method that improves fixed-prefix KL but slows down every update may still be valuable in a safety-critical experiment; the appropriate comparator depends on the quality requirement and system budget.

    9.3 Probe the mechanism before generalizing it

    Measure the projected residual along a fixed gradient or a small set of checkpoint directions, with matched prefix samples, before interpreting a quadratic fit as proof of feedback. Use several synchronization intervals and learning rates at the same nominal distance, plus an intervention that resets just the rollout weights. Re-evaluate the discrepancy on the same prefixes after changing reference version; a large jump there would contradict the practical fixed-offset model even though the three-policy factorization still holds. For head-size sensitivity, report the tail mass and occasionally estimate its weighted score on a small diagnostic vocabulary subset. This can reveal whether a lower or higher top-HHH is justified by actual score error rather than by an arbitrary token count.

    One can choose a budget for comparing approaches before tuning: wall time to a held-out score band with an uncertainty target, or model tokens consumed before a fixed-reference deviation threshold. Report both raw KL and its noise-floor ratio. Above all, keep equal-version engine differences visible in the accounting. A zero version gap with probability errors from precision, normalization or batching is a different state from a genuinely on-policy rollout.

    10. Reproduction plan and arithmetic checks

    The paper’s main result can be tested with a four-cell intervention: aligned, quantized only, stale only, and combined. Specify the sampler and trainer precision, draw and admission rules, exact versions, evaluation prefixes, group baseline and token reduction. Before comparing methods, measure three log-probabilities at common positions and verify that their split closes at numerical tolerance. Report paired on-policy seeds so that “one times the floor” is reproducible. Then compare drift and prefix/continuation residual proxies on matched checkpoints, and use fresh trainer rollouts to measure exploration and collapse. No model training was rerun for this review.

    For arithmetic checks, I enumerated the two-token score example, verified the sign and finite-difference limit of the logit gradient, and computed the Gaussian crossing, the estimated tail excess, and table differences. A useful test of the centering loss on a small vocabulary is to replace advantages by a fixed constant and compare the expectation before and after subtraction. A useful test of the resynchronization derivation is to set p=rp=rp=r without setting q=rq=rq=r: the lag should vanish, while the numerical discrepancy can remain. These tests check the equations and assumptions in the review; they do not establish the paper’s model-level outcomes.

    11. Conclusion

    DLC makes a helpful distinction between correcting a statistic of the current batch and changing what will be sampled next. The reference forward identifies a version ratio that can be clipped independently of numerical error; subtracting its weighted mean handles the constant-advantage part of the remaining update; resynchronization changes the future sampling law once overlap degrades. This sequence explains why simple centering works in the paper’s FP8 setting and fails in a frozen rollout, and why a stable fixed-prefix KL is insufficient evidence of a healthy policy. The next question for a larger system is whether this extra reference information and the adaptive refresh pay for themselves at equal time and target quality.
