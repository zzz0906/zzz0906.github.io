    AutoMaster: Improving What a Fixed Teacher Demonstrates

    Review date: 2026-11-23 · Author: Zhongzhu Zhou
    Paper reviewed: AutoMaster: Autonomous Post-Training by Making the Same Model a Better Teacher
    Paper authors: Guoxin Chen, Shuang Sun, Fanzhe Meng, Xinyu Tang, Ruihua Song, Wayne Xin Zhao
    arXiv: 2611.26510 · Version and date: v2, 2026-11-20; first submitted 2026-11-19

    1. The contribution I take away

    A teacher can solve a task while demonstrating a poor way to solve it. A successful search can contain a long chain of guesses; a passing numerical program can be accompanied by an unjustified accuracy claim. Filtering demonstrations by the final success label preserves those habits. AutoMaster asks whether a fixed teacher can produce more useful training data when natural-language skills change its behavior during generation.

    The paper separates Skill-FT, the recipe for generating and learning from skill-guided trajectories, from AutoMaster, the agent that develops skills and curates the resulting data. The teacher weights remain fixed. A student receives full-parameter supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and each experimental round restarts from the same student checkpoint. What accumulates across rounds is experiment history, skills, and selection decisions. The reported experiment is therefore an autonomous search over training interventions, rather than a sequence of five cumulative student updates.

    Figure 1: Original diagram based on paper Sections 3.1–3.3. Skills change the distribution of demonstrations; student feedback changes the next intervention. Restarting student weights makes the data intervention easier to interpret.

    Three observations make the paper useful. First, the same teacher and 512-example SFT budget leave measurable headroom beyond successful unguided trajectories. Second, selection and guidance interact: an isolated component can underperform the simple baseline. Third, retaining the teacher’s skill instructions in the training context helps even though those instructions disappear at evaluation. The last finding challenges a mechanical rule that training prompts should always be made to look as close as possible to evaluation prompts.

    My assessment is conditional. The strongest reported paired evidence is for the 9B student; two of the three 4B intervals cross zero. The feedback loop also repeatedly evaluates the named benchmarks and selects a checkpoint from that history. This is evidence for a promising data-development procedure under the reported protocol, with an unresolved question about performance on an untouched final test. I read all 21 pages of v2, including Appendix A’s training and uncertainty details and Appendix B’s four skill examples. Calculations below are explanatory derivations or arithmetic checks, not new model experiments.

    2. Prerequisites: trajectories, selection, and SFT

    2.1 A trajectory contains more than its final answer

    Let xxx be a task, ppp its base system prompt, and sss a skill or concatenation of applicable skills. At interaction step ttt, an agent produces an action or message ata_tat​ given its history hth_tht​; the environment returns observation oto_tot​. A rollout contains reasoning, tool calls, tool results, and final responses. A useful factorization is

    qT(τ∣x,p,s)=∏t=1TπT(at∣ht,p⊕s) P(ot∣ht,at).(1)q_T(\tau\mid x,p,s)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_T(a_t\mid h_t,p\oplus s)\,P(o_t\mid h_t,a_t). \tag{1}qT​(τ∣x,p,s)=t=1∏T​πT​(at​∣ht​,p⊕s)P(ot​∣ht​,at​).(1)

    Here PPP represents the environment and πT\pi_TπT​ the fixed teacher. This is my expansion of the paper’s rollout notation, not an additional assumption that environment observations receive a language-model training loss. Skills act through the teacher’s conditional decisions; they do not directly change PPP. In a stochastic environment, different outcomes can occur even for similar decisions, so one should avoid equating one successful trace with a universally reliable policy.

    Suppose R(τ)R(\tau)R(τ) is a success indicator and B(τ)B(\tau)B(τ) measures a behavior such as checking an independent reference. Successful-trajectory filtering samples from

    qsucc(τ∣x)=qT(τ∣x) 1{R(τ)=1}Pr⁡qT(R=1∣x).(2)q_{\mathrm{succ}}(\tau\mid x)=\frac{q_T(\tau\mid x)\,\mathbf{1}\{R(\tau)=1\}}{\Pr_{q_T}(R=1\mid x)}. \tag{2}qsucc​(τ∣x)=PrqT​​(R=1∣x)qT​(τ∣x)1{R(τ)=1}​.(2)

    The denominator must be positive. Conditioning on R=1R=1R=1 cannot distinguish robust and brittle successful paths unless the success criterion itself measures that distinction. Increasing the number of sampled successes can reduce sampling noise while preserving this bias in what gets taught. Guidance instead changes qTq_TqT​ before the success filter is applied.

    Figure 2: Original illustrative distribution, not paper measurements. Both teachers succeed on 80% of rollouts, but robust successes rise from 10% to 40% of all rollouts. Among successes, their share rises from 12.5% to 50%.

    The example helps identify what must be measured. A success-rate-only metric sees no change, while the student’s training distribution can change substantially. Conversely, observing a behavior more often does not prove that the behavior causes downstream gains. A verbose check can consume tokens without exposing a useful failure mode. Behavior quality needs an operational definition, not just a preference for longer trajectories.

    2.2 Which tokens receive supervision?

    Appendix A.3 says that assistant reasoning, responses, and tool calls receive cross-entropy loss. System, user, and tool messages remain context and are excluded. Write mit∈{0,1}m_{it}\in\{0,1\}mit​∈{0,1} for the supervision mask of token ttt in example iii, and citc_{it}cit​ for the corresponding prefix. A token-normalized objective is

    L(θ;D)=−1M∑i,tmitlog⁡πθ(yit∣cit),M=∑i,tmit.(3)\mathcal L(\theta;D)=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i,t}m_{it}\log\pi_\theta(y_{it}\mid c_{it}),\qquad M=\sum_{i,t}m_{it}. \tag{3}L(θ;D)=−M1​i,t∑​mit​logπθ​(yit​∣cit​),M=i,t∑​mit​.(3)

    This explains a subtlety: loss-masked is not causally inactive. Skill tokens need not be prediction targets to affect hidden states, attention, or gradients for later assistant tokens. Tool observations likewise teach the student how to act given an observation without training it to fabricate that observation as its own output.

    For a vocabulary logit vector zzz at one supervised token with target index yyy, expanding the negative log probability gives ℓ=−zy+log⁡∑jezj\ell=-z_y+\log\sum_j e^{z_j}ℓ=−zy​+log∑j​ezj​. Differentiation yields

    ∂ℓ∂zj=πθ(j∣c)−1{j=y}.(4)\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_j}=\pi_\theta(j\mid c)-\mathbf{1}\{j=y\}. \tag{4}∂zj​∂ℓ​=πθ​(j∣c)−1{j=y}.(4)

    Changing ccc by retaining a skill changes the probabilities and the network Jacobian feeding this gradient, even when yyy is identical. Keep and Remove therefore share targets but not the same learning problem. This elementary observation is enough to make their different outcomes plausible; it does not determine which condition should generalize better.

    Token normalization also matters for fair comparison. Equal numbers of trajectories do not imply equal numbers of supervised tokens. With global token normalization, a 20,000-token trajectory contributes roughly ten times as many token losses as a 2,000-token one before considering correlations. The paper holds demonstration count and the SFT recipe fixed, but a stronger compute-matched claim would also report token lengths, truncation, and actual optimizer workload.

    3. Skill-FT: changing generation before fitting the student

    The paper’s first equation can be stated directly:

    τ∼Rollout⁡(πT,x;p⊕s).(5)\tau\sim\operatorname{Rollout}(\pi_T,x;p\oplus s). \tag{5}τ∼Rollout(πT​,x;p⊕s).(5)

    A skill can specify a principle, procedure, or piece of domain knowledge. It is appended to the system prompt and can be shared across tasks, restricted to a subset, or combined with another skill. The teacher then attempts the task normally through the harness. A useful skill does more than ask for care: it describes when a rule applies and what evidence distinguishes the right next action.

    For example, a numerical-checking skill preserves the requested error convention and bound, identifies an independent reference, and requires the pass/fail decision to reach an assertion. A search skill targets an unsupported restriction when repeated queries stop producing discriminating evidence. The procedures are different because the failure mechanisms are different. Applying a numerical checklist to every search query would add cost without addressing the search bottleneck.

    After guided generation, AutoMaster chooses a 512-trajectory dataset. Selected trajectories retain the original prompt, including skills, for SFT. Some selected trajectories may be unguided if they already contain useful behavior. A schematic mixture makes this flexibility explicit:

    qD(τ,x)∝∑sws(x) qT(τ∣x,p,s) A(τ,x,s).(6)q_D(\tau,x)\propto\sum_s w_s(x)\,q_T(\tau\mid x,p,s)\,A(\tau,x,s). \tag{6}qD​(τ,x)∝s∑​ws​(x)qT​(τ∣x,p,s)A(τ,x,s).(6)

    Here ws(x)w_s(x)ws​(x) describes which skill is assigned and AAA is a selection weight or acceptance indicator. This is my analytical model, not a loss or numeric selection formula proposed in the paper. Its purpose is to separate three knobs: task composition, behavior generation, and demonstration selection. The experiment changes these knobs jointly, so an aggregate gain should not be attributed to skill text alone.

    Why use skills rather than a stronger teacher?

    Holding the teacher fixed isolates a practical opportunity: improve the demonstrations available from an already chosen model. A stronger teacher, additional rollout budget, or process-reward filtering would be reasonable alternatives. They change different costs and capabilities. AutoMaster’s evidence shows improvement over its stated same-teacher baseline; it does not establish superiority over every possible use of the same total development budget.

    The approach can also fail. A skill can prescribe an incorrect rule, encourage expensive checking on trivial tasks, or bias selection toward easy cases. Narrow task-specific hints could improve training tasks while transferring poorly. A skill whose output relies on unavailable private information might create an artificial demonstration that the student cannot reproduce under the evaluation prompt. Pilot generation tests whether guidance changes the teacher; downstream evaluation tests a different question, whether the student benefits.

    The most useful design choice is therefore the separation of teacher behavior quality and student learnability. A teacher can demonstrate a sophisticated procedure that a 4B student cannot reliably absorb. A compact, correctly scoped procedure could be more learnable than a long expert trace. The paper’s two student sizes provide an initial indication that capacity affects how consistently these interventions help, but they do not yet map out this trade-off across architectures or sequence lengths.

    4. The inner and outer loops, step by step

    Algorithm 1. Develop and curate behavioral interventions

    This pseudocode summarizes Section 3.2 in my own notation. The paper does not prescribe a fixed number of pilot trials or a formal scalar score for every selection decision.

    Input: fixed task pool X, fixed teacher T, initial student traces,
       experiment history H, demonstration budget N = 512
1. Read tasks and unguided teacher/student trajectories.
2. Identify a harmful habit or a useful underrepresented behavior.
3. Write a skill with explicit scope and a discriminating procedure.
4. Assign the skill to relevant task subsets.
5. Run pilot teacher trajectories with the assigned skills.
6. Inspect behavior, task outcomes, and new failure modes.
7. Revise wording or scope; repeat pilots when evidence warrants it.
8. Scale promising interventions within the fixed task pool.
9. Select N demonstrations using outcomes and observed behaviors;
   allow useful unguided demonstrations when appropriate.
10. Save task IDs, original prompts, trajectories, and their lineage.
Output: D_r and linked intervention records

    Steps 3 and 4 are coupled. A correct rule applied in the wrong situation can be harmful. The interpreter example later in the paper illustrates this sharply: adding execute permission is relevant for direct invocation, but unnecessary when the documented command explicitly invokes Python. A generic rule to fix every missing execute bit would be overbroad.

    Step 9 also creates a selection issue. A dataset full of easily verified trajectories may look clean while underrepresenting hard planning. Matching task IDs reduces one source of this confounding but does not automatically match token budget, outcome quality, or the number of candidate trajectories explored before selection. These distinctions matter when interpreting the ablations.

    Algorithm 2. Run autonomous post-training experiments

    Input: initial student weights theta_0, task pool X,
       teacher T, five-round budget, experiment history H
1. For r = 1,...,5:
2.     D_r <- Algorithm 1(X, T, H), with 512 demonstrations.
3.     theta_r <- full-parameter SFT(theta_0, D_r).
4.     Evaluate the final-epoch checkpoint without generated skills.
5.     Record scores, task trajectories, checkpoint, D_r, and skills.
6.     Add this evidence to H for the next experimental decision.
7. Select a checkpoint using accumulated evaluation feedback.
Output: selected checkpoint and final experiment report

    The crucial assignment is

    θr=SFT⁡(θ0,Dr),r=1,…,5.(7)\theta_r=\operatorname{SFT}(\theta_0,D_r),\qquad r=1,\ldots,5. \tag{7}θr​=SFT(θ0​,Dr​),r=1,…,5.(7)

    It is paper Equation (2), with the reset emphasized. Replacing θ0\theta_0θ0​ with θr−1\theta_{r-1}θr−1​ would define a different algorithm: cumulative training would mix data effects with the state left by earlier rounds and would expose later candidates to a different effective training budget. Resetting weights makes comparisons of DrD_rDr​ more interpretable, although it incurs five separate training runs.

    The outer loop is a small search over experiments. Its state is not just a score table: linked histories let the agent ask whether an intended behavior was absent from the teacher, generated but discarded, or present in training yet absent in the student. Those three diagnoses call for different repairs. The first suggests better skills, the second better selection, and the third potentially a learnability, coverage, or training-budget problem. This diagnostic decomposition is a conceptual benefit even before considering benchmark gains.

    5. Infrastructure: preserving experimental lineage

    Figure 4 describes the infrastructure reported in the paper. The orchestrating agent accesses tasks and initial trajectories, submits teacher rollouts, and launches SFT through an MCP service from a Docker environment. Teacher rollouts call an API; student evaluation queries SGLang. Tools and verifiers execute in isolated task containers. This is a description of the paper, without examining repository internals.

    Figure 4: Paper Figure 3, cropped from v2 p. 5, Chen et al., CC BY 4.0. Checkpoints are written to storage shared by training and evaluation clusters; evaluation records remain linked to training data and skills.

    The execution controller waits for a checkpoint, schedules evaluation, and records stage progress. Shared checkpoint storage avoids an intermediate copy between clusters. Restarting a failed inference worker can resume evaluation without retraining. These details matter because an autonomous search needs to distinguish an intervention that performs poorly from an experiment that failed operationally.

    A useful experiment record can be represented as (sr,Dr,θr,Er)(s_r,D_r,\theta_r,E_r)(sr​,Dr​,θr​,Er​), where ErE_rEr​ contains task-level evaluation scores and traces. A missing edge in this chain weakens diagnosis: a good score without dataset lineage cannot tell the agent which behavior transferred; a skill without a record of selected trajectories cannot tell whether it was actually taught. The system’s value is partly its ability to preserve this chain across asynchronous jobs.

    The design has costs. Full shared storage access and a sandbox cluster are infrastructure requirements, not evidence that the procedure is cheap on a small machine. Recovery must also preserve the distinction between a replay of the same evaluation and an additional stochastic evaluation. Otherwise, selective retries could silently change reported means. The paper describes recovery machinery, but does not provide an end-to-end price or wall-clock comparison that would establish a deployment break-even point.

    6. Experimental contract: what is held fixed

    Each domain has a fixed pool of 1,000 training tasks. Terminal uses CalibForge; Search uses FORT-Searcher; software engineering uses DeNovoSWE. Evaluation uses 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks, the 185-task BrowseComp-Lite subset released by Traverse, and 50 Doc2Repo repositories. Search tools are disabled for the software-engineering evaluation harness. BrowseComp-Lite results should not be silently presented as results on the full BrowseComp benchmark.

    The students are Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B. The reported orchestrator runs GPT-6 Astra in Codex at xhigh reasoning effort; the teacher is DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Preview at high reasoning effort. These are the paper’s experimental settings. A cheaper orchestrator or weaker teacher may change the intervention quality, and has not been tested by this review.

    Every SFT run uses 512 trajectories, three epochs, global batch size 32, full-parameter updates, and a maximum sequence length of 131,072 tokens. Appendix A.3 specifies Megatron Adam, learning rate 10−510^{-5}10−5 decreasing to 10−610^{-6}10−6 with a cosine schedule, 5% warmup, β1=0.9\beta_1=0.9β1​=0.9, β2=0.999\beta_2=0.999β2​=0.999, zero weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, and bfloat16 training. System, user, and tool tokens are excluded from the loss.

    A simple count gives

    Unominal=512×332=48(8)U_{\mathrm{nominal}}=\frac{512\times3}{32}=48 \tag{8}Unominal​=32512×3​=48(8)

    example-batch updates per round if the global batch is measured in trajectories, without additional packing or partial-batch complications. Five rounds would then imply 240 such updates. This is an accounting illustration, not a measured optimizer-step trace. With very long examples, token work can vary greatly at the same example count.

    Teacher generation uses temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0. Maximum response completions are 16,384 tokens for Terminal/SWE and 8,192 for Search, with step limits 500/400/300 respectively. Teacher task limits are one hour for Terminal and three hours for SWE; Search has no overall time limit. Evaluation uses temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, at most 65,500 tokens per response, and 600 steps; Terminal has a three-hour limit and SWE four hours. All methods share the same evaluation settings within a domain.

    These large ceilings are caps, not proof that every example consumes the maximum. They do make actual token and latency distributions essential for a practical cost assessment. An agent that learns to check more carefully could score better while becoming much slower. Task accuracy and repository pass rate do not resolve that trade-off by themselves.

    7.1 What three evaluation runs mean

    The reported standard deviations describe three evaluation runs of a fixed checkpoint. They do not measure variance across independently trained students, independently developed skill libraries, or independently orchestrated five-round searches. This distinction matters especially for small gains: a low evaluation SD does not establish that the training procedure is stable across seeds.

    Appendix A.6 supplies task-paired bootstrap intervals. Let vijAv_{ij}^{A}vijA​ and vijVv_{ij}^{V}vijV​ be scores for task iii, evaluation repetition jjj, for AutoMaster and Vanilla. The task-level difference is

    di=13∑j=13(vijA−vijV),Δ^=100n∑i=1ndi.(9)d_i=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}(v_{ij}^{A}-v_{ij}^{V}),\qquad \widehat\Delta=\frac{100}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_i. \tag{9}di​=31​j=1∑3​(vijA​−vijV​),Δ=n100​i=1∑n​di​.(9)

    Scores here are fractions; multiplication by 100 expresses percentage points. Pairing is at the task level after averaging repetitions. It does not imply that stochastic runs used paired random seeds.

    For bootstrap replicate bbb, sample nnn task indices with replacement and preserve the two methods’ scores for each sampled task:

    Δ^(b)=100n∑k=1ndIk(b),Ik(b)∼Uniform⁡{1,…,n}.(10)\widehat\Delta^{(b)}=\frac{100}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{I_k^{(b)}},\quad I_k^{(b)}\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,n\}. \tag{10}Δ(b)=n100​k=1∑n​dIk(b)​​,Ik(b)​∼Uniform{1,…,n}.(10)

    The paper uses 10,000 resamples, seed 0, and percentile endpoints 2.5% and 97.5%. For Doc2Repo, the unit is a whole repository, not an individual test within it. Resampling tests independently would treat correlated tests as independent evidence and produce misleadingly narrow intervals.

    For Terminal 4B/9B the intervals are [1.12, 10.49] and [2.25, 16.10]. For Search they are [−1.08, 6.85] and [0.72, 8.83]. For SWE they are [−1.94, 5.33] and [0.65, 8.93]. Thus all three 9B lower bounds are positive; Search 4B and SWE 4B remain compatible with no gain under this analysis. These are pointwise intervals, without a stated simultaneous six-comparison guarantee.

    The intervals also condition on the reported selected checkpoints. Because the system uses benchmark feedback to choose experiments and a final checkpoint, a genuinely final estimate would require a held-out evaluation layer. A paired bootstrap of already observed task scores cannot retroactively turn adaptive development data into untouched test data.

    8. Ablations: guidance and selection are complements

    The two component removals answer different questions. Without skill guidance, the agent selects from unguided teacher rollouts. Without data selection, it develops skills on a fixed randomly sampled task subset but omits agent-directed trajectory selection. Both retain autonomous experimentation under the same stated teacher, task pool, demonstration count, and SFT recipe.

    Figure 6: Redrawn from paper Table A4. These are mean scores, without SD bars in this redraw. The paper supplies mean ± SD. The two rows separate 4B and 9B; SWE uses pass rate rather than accuracy.

    Terminal 4B is the most informative counterexample to a blanket “skills always help” reading. Vanilla SFT scores 22.47; selection without guidance gives 17.60; guidance without selection gives 20.22; the full method gives 28.09. Both isolated variants underperform Vanilla, while the combination improves substantially. This pattern suggests complementarity in this setting, but the interventions are not a perfectly balanced factorial experiment that identifies one universal interaction coefficient.

    On Search 4B, selection without guidance gives 45.77, close to the full 46.13, while guidance without selection gives 43.78. One plausible interpretation is that useful search behavior already exists in unguided rollouts and can be selected. Terminal may need behavior that is rarer and must first be elicited. This is a hypothesis consistent with the table, not a demonstrated mechanism shared by every task.

    Task-matched data is a useful but incomplete control

    Task-Matched SFT preserves AutoMaster’s final task IDs and per-task counts but substitutes unguided teacher trajectories. AutoMaster exceeds it in all six means, by 1.08–10.11 points. The comparison rules out a narrow explanation based solely on final task identity and sampling counts.

    However, Appendix A.5 explicitly retains both successful and unsuccessful unguided trajectories to preserve that match. In the Terminal 4B corpus, 106 of 512 unguided trajectories fail while their matched AutoMaster demonstrations succeed; the other 406 succeed in both conditions. The corresponding failure-to-success counts are 100 for Terminal 9B, 45 for Search 4B, and 64 for Search 9B. Therefore

    fboth succeed∈{406512,412512,467512,448512}≈{79.3,80.5,91.2,87.5}%.(11)f_{\mathrm{both\ succeed}}\in\left\{\frac{406}{512},\frac{412}{512},\frac{467}{512},\frac{448}{512}\right\}\approx\{79.3,80.5,91.2,87.5\}\%. \tag{11}fboth succeed​∈{512406​,512412​,512467​,512448​}≈{79.3,80.5,91.2,87.5}%.(11)

    Most matched pairs succeed in both conditions, which makes behavioral differences relevant. Yet 8.8–20.7% outcome differences are large enough that the downstream comparison is not a pure estimate of behavior quality conditional on success. Training only the successful–successful matched pairs, with equal tokens and equal sample counts, would isolate that narrower question more directly.

    SWE is less binary. For the 4B corpus, guided demonstrations improve/retain/decrease task scores in 43/433/36 pairs; for 9B, the counts are 24/483/5. This exposes a real boundary: guidance can worsen some demonstrations. A curation procedure that assumes every guided trace is better would discard useful evidence of regressions.

    9. Reading the behavioral examples as evidence

    The paper’s cases are useful because they distinguish the acceptance criterion from the reasoning that produced a solution. They are selected examples, however, rather than a prevalence estimate of each failure mode. I treat them as concrete mechanisms that could explain part of the aggregate gains.

    Figure 7: Paper Figure 5, cropped from v2 p. 9, Chen et al., CC BY 4.0. The examples concern numerical claims, evidence-guided search, and diagnosis of a faulty self-test.

    Numerical accuracy: passing a threshold does not prove an order

    The unguided Poisson solver satisfies the requested absolute-error threshold yet claims fourth-order accuracy. Its errors are approximately 1.36×10−31.36\times10^{-3}1.36×10−3 at grid size 32 and 3.31×10−43.31\times10^{-4}3.31×10−4 at size 64. If asymptotic error satisfies E(h)≈ChpE(h)\approx Ch^pE(h)≈Chp, halving the spacing gives

    E(h)E(h/2)≈2p,p≈log⁡(E(h)/E(h/2))log⁡2≈2.04.(12)\frac{E(h)}{E(h/2)}\approx2^p,\qquad p\approx\frac{\log(E(h)/E(h/2))}{\log2}\approx2.04. \tag{12}E(h/2)E(h)​≈2p,p≈log2log(E(h)/E(h/2))​≈2.04.(12)

    This arithmetic supports approximately second-order convergence, subject to the asymptotic-regime assumption. One error below 5×10−45\times10^{-4}5×10−4 verifies the requested threshold at that configuration, not a fourth-order rate. The guided example uses an independent check, repairs the method with a nine-point stencil, and reports fourth-order behavior using direct solves across grids. I have not rerun either solver; the convergence calculation here uses the numbers printed in the paper.

    This distinction generalizes. Correctness at one input, asymptotic accuracy, numerical stability, and runtime are different claims with different evidence. A helpful skill ties each claim to the relevant measurement. It should not silently strengthen the task requirement or reject a valid cheaper second-order solver merely because a higher-order method exists.

    Search: a pivot must change the unsupported assumption

    Both search trajectories identify the same television episode. The unguided teacher repeatedly guesses an award. The guided trace uses episode–director evidence to identify a candidate and then verifies education and award clues. The intervention changes the order of evidence acquisition, rather than supplying the answer as a hidden hint.

    The failure boundary is premature narrowing. A guessed award becomes a query restriction; repeated searches then mostly retrieve material consistent with that guess. A useful pivot relaxes the unsupported restriction and seeks an independent relation capable of discriminating candidates. Rephrasing the same restricted query does not necessarily supply new evidence. The paper illustrates this mechanism but does not report a controlled rate of genuine pivots across the full corpus.

    UTF-16 ordering: diagnose the test before changing the rule

    The software example compares U+FB33 with emoji U+1F602 under ascending UTF-16BE ordering. The emoji begins with surrogate code unit D83D, so D83D precedes FB33. The mistaken self-test instead uses U+05D3 followed by U+05BC. Its first code unit, 05D3, precedes D83D. Thus the two strings have different correct positions:

    05D3<D83D<FB33.(13)\mathtt{05D3}<\mathtt{D83D}<\mathtt{FB33}. \tag{13}05D3<D83D<FB33.(13)

    Reversing the global sorting rule to satisfy the mistaken expectation breaks the specification. The guided teacher repairs the test input and preserves ascending order, passing 38/38 tests versus 32/38 in the unguided example. This is a reported trajectory outcome, not a repository result validated by this review. The larger lesson is to distinguish an implementation defect from a faulty oracle before changing either.

    10. Why retaining skill context can help

    Keep retains generated skills in SFT inputs; Remove deletes only those instructions. Supervised reasoning, responses, and tool calls remain the same. Evaluation supplies no generated skills in either condition. The six reported Keep-minus-Remove gains are 3.00/3.75 points on Terminal, 3.60/2.34 on Search, and 4.01/2.15 on SWE, with 4B/9B ordering.

    Figure 8: Paper Figure 6, cropped from v2 p. 10, Chen et al., CC BY 4.0. Dots are evaluation runs for fixed checkpoints; bars show means and SD. Retaining guidance helps all six reported means despite its absence at evaluation.

    10.1 Two different conditional learning problems

    With task prompt xxx, skill sss, and assistant outputs yyy, the two objectives differ in their conditioning context:

    LK=−EDlog⁡πθ(y∣x,s),LR=−EDlog⁡πθ(y∣x).(14)\mathcal L_K=-\mathbb E_{D}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x,s),\qquad \mathcal L_R=-\mathbb E_{D}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x). \tag{14}LK​=−ED​logπθ​(y∣x,s),LR​=−ED​logπθ​(y∣x).(14)

    This shorthand omits the token mask already defined in Equation (3). The data distribution over (x,s,y)(x,s,y)(x,s,y) is the same, but Remove hides sss. Under ideal conditional maximum likelihood with unlimited capacity, marginalizing skills would involve

    pD(y∣x)=∑spD(s∣x) pD(y∣x,s).(15)p_D(y\mid x)=\sum_s p_D(s\mid x)\,p_D(y\mid x,s). \tag{15}pD​(y∣x)=s∑​pD​(s∣x)pD​(y∣x,s).(15)

    If different skills explain different valid actions, the skill-free conditional can be harder to learn. The usual entropy identity makes the information difference explicit:

    HD(Y∣X)−HD(Y∣X,S)=ID(Y;S∣X)≥0.(16)H_D(Y\mid X)-H_D(Y\mid X,S)=I_D(Y;S\mid X)\ge0. \tag{16}HD​(Y∣X)−HD​(Y∣X,S)=ID​(Y;S∣X)≥0.(16)

    This is a general identity, not a theorem establishing AutoMaster’s test-time generalization. At evaluation the skill is absent, so a model trained only on the richer context could also become dependent on it. The empirical result says that, in these six settings, the benefit of retaining the context outweighs that risk in mean score. It does not prove a universal advantage for adding training-only context.

    10.2 Coherence is a concrete alternative explanation

    Some demonstrations explicitly reference the supplied skill. Remove leaves those references in the supervised output while deleting their antecedent from the prompt. The interpreter example uses a conditional rule: direct execution may require permission, while explicit interpreter invocation does not. The parser example derives precedence from a supplied grammar. Deleting the original rules can make the target trace less well explained by its context.

    Keep may therefore help by preserving input–output coherence, by easing conditional rule learning, or by changing attention and gradient statistics. The paper offers illustrative support for the first two, but does not identify a unique causal mechanism. Calling the gain proof of reasoning internalization would be stronger than the evidence supports.

    A sharper experiment would include a coherent Remove condition that rewrites only skill-dependent references while preserving the substantive actions, plus a paraphrased Keep condition, a length-matched irrelevant-context control, and skill dropout. If Keep’s advantage survives removal of orphaned references and irrelevant-token controls, the case for learning conditional procedures becomes stronger. These are proposed experiments, not results obtained here.

    The application boundary is also clear. Retaining accurate, scoped procedures can be useful; retaining an incorrect rule can teach a systematic error. Evaluation without skills should include counterexamples where the skill’s precondition is false, so a model that blindly executes a memorized procedure does not receive credit for genuine conditional transfer.

    11. Five rounds: exploration, selection, and cost

    Figure 9 shows the outer-loop trajectories. The best observed means occur in rounds two through four, improving on the first round by 2.16–4.87 points across the six settings. BrowseComp 9B stays near 49% for the first three rounds before reaching 51.53% in round four. Later rounds need not improve monotonically because they explore different interventions and restart student weights.

    Figure 9: Paper Figure 8, cropped from v2 p. 11, Chen et al., CC BY 4.0. Dashed lines are Vanilla SFT means. Every point comes from a student trained from the initial checkpoint, and filled markers identify the best observed mean.

    The 9B model exceeds Vanilla’s mean in every round in all three domains. The 4B model does so in all five rounds only for Terminal. This is consistent with greater capacity making useful behaviors easier to absorb, but model size is not experimentally isolated from every other capacity-related effect. It would be premature to infer a scaling law from two sizes.

    A useful cost decomposition is

    Cdev=Cinitial+∑r=15(Cagent,r+Cpilot,r+Cbatch,r+CSFT,r+Ceval,r).(17)C_{\mathrm{dev}}=C_{\mathrm{initial}}+\sum_{r=1}^{5}\left(C_{\mathrm{agent},r}+C_{\mathrm{pilot},r}+C_{\mathrm{batch},r}+C_{\mathrm{SFT},r}+C_{\mathrm{eval},r}\right). \tag{17}Cdev​=Cinitial​+r=1∑5​(Cagent,r​+Cpilot,r​+Cbatch,r​+CSFT,r​+Ceval,r​).(17)

    Equal final training-set size controls only part of this expression. An autonomous agent can spend substantial compute discovering which 512 examples to keep. A fair operational comparison should specify whether the alternative baseline receives the same teacher-token, training, evaluation, dollar, or elapsed-time budget. These constraints can favor different methods.

    For intuition, suppose a system contains 512 examples with average supervised length 12,000 tokens. Three epochs expose about 18.4 million supervised tokens; at 24,000 average tokens, exposure doubles despite unchanged example count. These are illustrative assumptions, not reported corpus lengths. Prompt tokens and tool context further affect compute without necessarily increasing supervised tokens. This is why a token-level ledger would make the paper’s resource claim more informative.

    Adaptive selection creates another issue. A simple model writes an observed score as true score plus noise, V^r=Vr+ϵr\widehat V_r=V_r+\epsilon_rVr​=Vr​+ϵr​. Choosing r∗=arg⁡max⁡rV^rr^*=\arg\max_r\widehat V_rr∗=argmaxr​Vr​ can select a positive noise realization as well as a better intervention. Here rounds also use earlier task feedback to choose future data, so the concern includes adaptation to benchmark tasks, not merely taking the largest of five independent noisy numbers. More search can improve a method and increase the need for untouched final evaluation at the same time.

    12. Limitations and failure boundaries

    Development and evaluation are coupled. Appendix A.4 says benchmark scores and trajectories feed subsequent experimentation, and checkpoint selection uses accumulated evaluation feedback. The reported results are meaningful for that protocol, but do not isolate final out-of-sample generalization. No specific data leakage incident is established by this observation; the methodological issue is repeated adaptive access to evaluation evidence.

    Uncertainty covers a restricted layer. Three runs characterize evaluation randomness for fixed checkpoints. The paired bootstrap characterizes task/repository variation conditional on those recorded evaluations. Neither measures variation across SFT seeds, skill-generation searches, or checkpoint selection. Two 4B intervals include zero; six pointwise intervals do not constitute a familywise guarantee.

    Behavioral evidence is selective. Figure 5 and Appendix B show plausible, inspectable examples. They do not measure how often a skill causes a desirable behavior, how many unguided successes were already robust, or how frequently skills introduce regressions. A large language model can produce more verification language without doing a more informative verification action.

    Matched tasks do not imply matched demonstration quality. Task-Matched SFT includes failed unguided examples. It controls task composition while leaving outcome quality different. The successful–successful majority makes a behavioral explanation plausible, but the aggregate gain cannot be assigned entirely to that majority without a separate controlled experiment.

    Generalization across systems remains open. The experiments use one teacher, one orchestration configuration, two student sizes from one family, and three task domains. Different teachers may already demonstrate the desired behaviors, and weaker students may lack the capacity or context budget to absorb them. A shorter deployment context can remove evidence that the long training trace relied on.

    Cost and latency are incompletely characterized. Fixed trajectory count and a common SFT recipe do not equalize total search compute, corpus tokens, teacher rollouts, or test-time action count. The result establishes score improvements over named baselines, while leaving a score–cost frontier unresolved.

    Skills can encode task knowledge. Appendix B’s grammar skill includes domain-specific rules. That is allowed by the method, but it means the intervention can carry knowledge as well as general strategy. A test of broad behavioral improvement should distinguish transferable procedures from task-family-specific factual or formal hints.

    13. Critical analysis: the experiments I would change first

    13.1 Separate intervention development from final assessment

    My first change would be a three-way protocol: training tasks for demonstration generation, development tasks for the five-round feedback loop, and a sealed test set used once after checkpoint selection. Benchmark task traces should remain outside the agent’s view until the final test is fixed. The point is not to prohibit useful feedback; it is to measure what that feedback buys on tasks it did not directly inform.

    With limited benchmark sizes, cross-fitting across task folds is an alternative, provided each fold’s final evaluation is performed by a system developed without that fold. It is more expensive than a single split, but more informative than treating a bootstrap over reused task scores as a complete generalization estimate. Report the search budget and selection rule before looking at the sealed results.

    13.2 Make behavior a measurable mediator

    Define specific behaviors before comparing methods: an independent numerical reference that can reject the current result; a search query that relaxes an unsupported restriction; or a test whose expectation is derived from the actual representation. Annotate a random held-out sample of both successful and failed traces, with annotators blind to the method. Measure desirable behavior, unnecessary actions, and regressions together.

    Then vary guidance while holding task identities, successful outcomes, and token budgets as close as feasible. A student trained on a success-matched corpus can help distinguish teaching better procedures from simply supplying more correct answers. For SWE, preserve repository-level grouping because many tests share the same implementation and failure cause. An action-level behavioral measure is useful only if it predicts future task reliability rather than reward-model preference for polished prose.

    13.3 Disentangle context coherence from general rule learning

    The Keep–Remove result deserves its own carefully controlled study. Retaining a skill supplies extra input tokens and restores antecedents of explicit references. A clean comparison should distinguish correct procedural content, generic verbosity, formal knowledge, and the coherence of the target trace. Evaluate with no skills, paraphrased skills, contradictory skills, and tasks where a skill’s condition is deliberately false.

    One proposed training mixture would retain a skill with probability ρ\rhoρ and use a coherently rewritten skill-free trace otherwise. Varying ρ\rhoρ would test dependency versus internalization. This is a different method and is not advocated as an established improvement. Its value is diagnostic: if a small amount of skill-free training preserves the benefit and reduces incorrect rule activation, the original effect becomes easier to deploy responsibly.

    13.4 Compare search procedures at equal budgets

    At the same total development budget, compare AutoMaster with additional successful unguided rollouts, a simpler fixed skill library, random skill–task assignments, and human-designed scoped skills. Track teacher input/output tokens, failed pilots, SFT tokens, evaluation calls, agent decisions, and wall time. Include the value of existing infrastructure separately from the marginal cost of a new task family.

    The agent’s sophisticated reasoning could be the expensive part, or repeated SFT/evaluation could dominate. Without the breakdown, a team cannot know whether AutoMaster is attractive for a one-off 4B model or for a pipeline reused over many students. My favorable reading of the paper is therefore about where to intervene in data creation; my main reservation concerns how completely the protocol measures transferable gain per unit of development effort.

    14. Reproducibility priorities and checks made here

    A paper-based reproduction plan starts by fixing task splits, the teacher version, original student checkpoint, harness behavior, prompt templates, supervision masks, and the five-round selection policy. Preserve the original skill-bearing system prompt with every trajectory. Record whether a demonstration is guided, the skill’s task scope, its outcome, and why it was selected. The lineage must connect the data actually used in SFT to the checkpoint actually evaluated.

    Next, match the training recipe from Appendix A.3 and evaluation settings from A.4. Distinguish an end-of-third-epoch checkpoint from a checkpoint selected across experimental rounds. Report task accuracy for Terminal/Search and repository-mean test pass rate for SWE. Retain all tasks, including failures and timeouts, and resample whole repositories for the SWE interval. Treat operational retries consistently rather than dropping inconvenient failures.

    I independently checked the printed score differences, the successful-pair proportions, the 48 nominal updates under the stated counting assumption, the second-order convergence calculation, the UTF-16 code-unit ordering, and a small softmax-gradient finite difference. Those checks validate the explanatory arithmetic. They do not reproduce benchmark scores, establish skill generalization, or validate the authors’ software.

    15. Conclusion

    AutoMaster makes a compelling practical point: the value of a teacher is partly determined by the behavior its demonstrations expose. Natural-language skills can alter that behavior before training data is selected, and retaining the explanatory context during SFT can help students even when the context is absent later. The joint selection–guidance result is more persuasive than a claim that adding skills is uniformly beneficial.

    I would use the paper to motivate a measured demonstration-development experiment with explicit behavior labels and a held-out final test. The 9B results provide consistent paired evidence under the reported protocol; the smaller 4B effects and adaptive feedback loop limit broader conclusions. The next useful result would combine the score gains with independent search repetitions, success-matched controls, and a complete development-cost ledger.
