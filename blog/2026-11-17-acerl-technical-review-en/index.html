    ACE-RL: Training the Experience an Agent Hands to Its Next Attempt

    Review date: 2026-11-17
    Author: Zhongzhu Zhou
    Paper reviewed: Experience-Handoff Reinforcement Learning for Recursive Self-Improvement
    Paper authors: Doyoung Kim, Byeongguk Jeon, Kailin Zhang, Sherry Yang
    arXiv: 2611.16409v1, 2026-11-12

    1. The unit of training is a handoff

    Consider an agent that fails an API task because it looked at the wrong collection of transactions. Its final reward is zero. The trace contains the query, the empty result, and the action it took after seeing that result. Starting from scratch, a new rollout can repeat the same search with different wording. With a playbook, it can start from a specific premise that the earlier attempt already tested. The learning problem is making the premise faithful enough to survive compression and concrete enough to change the next action.

    Kim et al. put a single set of model weights behind three fixed role prompts. The actor interacts with the environment. The reflector distills a trajectory into a diagnosis. The curator turns the diagnosis into operations on a small task-local playbook. The causal sequence is interesting because the output of one call becomes part of the prompt distribution for a later call from the same model. The supervised target for the reflector is absent. A candidate playbook is scored by resetting the episode, letting the actor read it, and taking the verifier’s result. The evaluator episode also trains the actor.

    Figure 1. Cropped from Kim et al., Fig. 1. The extra rollout is where edits acquire a task reward in training. At inference the next normal visit consumes the edit directly.

    The paper’s meaning of recursive self-improvement is deliberately operational. A model revises the text it sees on the next visit while the test-time weights and control program stay fixed. A new task starts with an empty playbook. This narrows the scientific claim to a form of adaptation with visible state and a controlled reset. It also means the experiments leave open whether memorized task details can become reusable skills or whether edits remain helpful across environments. The original Agentic Context Engineering paper is the scaffold reference. Here the actor and both text-writing behaviors are trained together.

    2. Prerequisites

    Episodes, policy inputs, and verifiers

    Let qqq denote a task instance. An episode starts by initializing its environment and may contain many turns. The model emits action tokens, receives observations, and eventually obtains a verifier reward Rq(τ)R_q(\tau)Rq​(τ) from the trajectory τ\tauτ. A visit is one such episode together with the surrounding handoff calls. A second visit can repeat the task from the same initial condition while reading an updated playbook. Environment state, prior tool output, and the old transcript do not become the next actor’s interactive state. The formatted playbook is the channel through which earlier discoveries reach that actor.

    A probability over a trajectory includes policy action probabilities and environment transition factors. With parameter-independent dynamics, differentiating the log probability leaves only the sum of the scores of generated actions. To see why a tool reply belongs in the conditioning context but contributes no score, write one turn as pθ(at∣ht)P(ot∣ht,at)p_\theta(a_t\mid h_t)P(o_t\mid h_t,a_t)pθ​(at​∣ht​)P(ot​∣ht​,at​). Its logarithm is the sum of model log probability and a transition term whose derivative in θ\thetaθ is zero. The same rule applies when the reflector sees that reply. Ordinary language tokens in the reflector or curator’s generated answer do have a score.

    Outcome-relative policy updates

    For actor-only GRPO, one collects siblings sharing a task prompt. A scalar score above the group mean yields a positive advantage. The training signal of each generated token in that sample inherits the same group-level advantage. If all sampled outcomes are identical, the centered comparison supplies zero signal. In the harness, both the task input and the playbook can influence the reward. Grouping reflections of a single trajectory controls the evidence they read. Grouping edits of a single reflection controls the proposed strategic change. The actor group deliberately trades away this same-input property, pooling incumbent and candidate prompts to reuse expensive episodes.

    Why task-local memory matters

    The playbook can retain facts as specific as what one guess established about a hidden code or which endpoint exposes a relationship in AppWorld. This makes a reset-and-retry protocol measurable and separates actions from stored text. It also changes what “generalization” can mean. A model can generalize the process of writing useful task-specific notes to held-out tasks while storing particular facts that apply to just one instance. To establish reusable procedural knowledge, the test would need a separate transfer step that removes identifiers and other instance-specific state.

    The online update trains the seed for its own outcome

    4. Sparse branching under the actor budget

    Figure 2. Cropped from Kim et al., Fig. 3. Branching among reflections holds the seed fixed. Branching among edits holds one reflection fixed. All admitted evaluation episodes return to the actor training group.

    At the main setting (N,M,C)=(4,2,3)(N,M,C)=(4,2,3)(N,M,C)=(4,2,3), four episodes sample the incumbent and one is picked uniformly for reflection. Two interpretations of that trajectory are generated. One usable interpretation is chosen uniformly to receive three curator samples, while the other usable interpretation supplies one. Each edit uses a copy of the same incumbent. An admissible edit must parse, apply, change the text, and stay within 2,048 tokens. The paper describes ordered ADD, UPDATE, and REMOVE operations, followed by deduplication and renumbering. With both reflections usable and every edit admitted, four evaluator episodes replace the remaining four actor-only episodes in the nominal GRPO budget. Actual accepted groups can be smaller.

    Algorithm 1. One ACE-RL training collection and update

    This pseudocode follows paper Algorithm 1 and Appendix D. “Usable” applies to the reflection. “Admissible” applies to a curator output after validation. The selected playbook and the training batch have separate lifecycles.

    01  Freeze the collection policy; start an empty update batch.
02  For each task, increment its encounter counter.
03  Before each reset encounter, restore the empty playbook.
04  Sample four seed episodes from the incumbent and score them.
05  Pick one seed uniformly; sample two reflections of that seed.
06  Pick one usable reflection uniformly for three curator samples.
07  Draw one edit for each other usable reflection.
08  Validate each edit against a copy of the incumbent.
09  For each admitted edit, reset the environment and score one retry.
10  Credit actors by own reward and editors by candidate reward.
11  Credit reflections by mean admitted values (zero if empty).
12  Retain a maximum-scoring candidate if it matches or beats the seed.
13  Keep group identifiers for actor, reflector and curator outputs.
14  Filter groups, normalize within groups, and update shared weights.

    Algorithm 2. Run the trained harness on a new task

    The additional playbook-ranking rollouts are confined to training. The test protocol applies the sampled edit and uses its effect when the next scheduled attempt arrives. This distinction matters when a candidate is cheap to author and slow to verify.

    01  Initialize a task-local empty playbook and best reward zero.
02  For each of K visits, start a fresh episode of this task.
03  Give the actor the task and current playbook; record its reward.
04  Update best reward with the maximum observed so far.
05  Give the reflector the formatted trace and verifier feedback.
06  Ask the curator for edit operations using the current playbook.
07  Apply the edit using the harness rules for the next visit.
08  Return best reward and retain the visit-level trace for analysis.

    5. Reward means, normalization, and selection

    A finite-group correction

    With independent siblings and conditional score mean zero, compare each value rir_iri​ with a leave-one-out mean b−ib_{-i}b−i​. Its expected product with the sample’s score is unchanged because the term involving other siblings vanishes conditional on the input.     ri−rˉ=G−1G(ri−b−i).r_i-\bar r=\frac{G-1}{G}(r_i-b_{-i}).ri​−rˉ=GG−1​(ri​−b−i​).

    The corresponding raw score estimate shrinks by 1/21/21/2 for a two-reflection group and by 2/32/32/3 for a three-curator group, assuming the raw independent-sibling setup. Dividing by the sampled standard deviation changes more than that scalar. For two non-tied samples, normalization substantially converts a magnitude comparison into a sign comparison. A change in the noise of a descendant value changes the chance of reversing that sign. The real actor group mixes playbook inputs, so the most direct same-input correction applies to the idealized groups, with additional qualifications for admission and mixed actor prompts.

    Admission changes the expected reflector credit

    The paper gives a useful example in Appendix D.1. Suppose edits are independently admitted with probability ppp and each admitted edit has expected value vvv. Conditional on a nonempty admitted set, the expected valid-only mean is vvv. With zero credited to an empty set, it follows that

    Filtering, clipping, and memory selection

    Per Appendix D.3, the comparison batch drops groups whose rewards are all zero or all one under the threshold rule. MiniSWEBench instead uses a nonzero range check for shaped rewards. A singleton with a fractional outcome can survive threshold filtering and receive an outcome-weighted update under the reported mean-zero, scale-one convention. Constant interior-reward groups with multiple outputs have zero centered advantage. These details qualify the main-text shorthand about dropping constant groups. No-op edits produce no candidate and carry zero edit reward. Some curator prompts permit valid no-ops when evidence supplies no improvement. Their structural validity as responses and their eligibility for downstream credit are distinct.

    For an advantage AAA and token ratio ρ\rhoρ between the current policy and collection policy, paper Eq. 24 uses

    ℓ0(ρ,A)=min⁡(ρA,clip⁡(ρ,0.8,1.28)A).\ell_0(\rho,A)=\min\bigl(\rho A,\operatorname{clip}(\rho,0.8,1.28)A\bigr).ℓ0​(ρ,A)=min(ρA,clip(ρ,0.8,1.28)A).

    For A<0A<0A<0, dual clipping uses max⁡(ℓ0,3A)\max(\ell_0,3A)max(ℓ0​,3A). This caps the negative term for an outsized ratio. The batch averages masked token losses over generated tokens from all retained roles. If an actor output and a reflector output have equal scalar advantages but different lengths, their token counts differ in the loss. When branches are filtered, both the numerator and denominator change. The paper reports one PPO epoch per batch, a learning rate of 10−610^{-6}10−6, and no KL or entropy bonus for ACE-RL. Those design choices belong to the empirical surrogate. Uniformly thinning an ideal unnormalized tree does not prove equivalence after these operations.

    The playbook-selection threshold compares the best observed candidate with the chosen seed’s own reward and retains the candidate on ties. Training resets before encounters 3, 6, and onward for each task. Group rewards remain eligible even if the incumbent is retained. Under an illustrative binary verifier, four independent candidates each with true success probability 0.30.30.3 supply a positive observed maximum with probability 1−0.74=0.75991-0.7^4=0.75991−0.74=0.7599. All four candidates can be objectively worse than the incumbent under this scenario. A count of retained changes alone would conflate that selection effect with true improvement.

    Value maximization and a capacity decision

    The sparse algorithm has three independently meaningful allocations. One can increase the number of distinct seed rollouts NNN, the number of reflections MMM, or the number of edits CCC under a chosen reflection. Within the all-valid regime, the actor cap is B=N+M+C−1B=N+M+C-1B=N+M+C−1. Increasing CCC at fixed BBB requires reducing seed rollouts or reflector breadth. If the verifier is noisy, spending a second evaluation on the same candidate reduces that leaf’s variance, but a fixed BBB then buys fewer candidates and fewer chances to obtain an admitted edit. With B=8B=8B=8 and N=4N=4N=4, replacing four single evaluations with two double evaluations would preserve the cap and reduce coverage of edits. It could also train the actor on duplicate playbook inputs, which changes its input diversity.

    A simple cost model makes the trade explicit. Let cec_ece​ denote average end-to-end cost of one evaluation episode and cz,cuc_z,c_ucz​,cu​ the costs of a reflection and an edit. Ignoring reset failures and scheduling overlap, the maximal collection cost at the main configuration is 4cs+2cz+4cu+4ce4c_s+2c_z+4c_u+4c_e4cs​+2cz​+4cu​+4ce​. GRPO spends eight seed costs. A budget comparison needs measured csc_scs​ and cec_ece​ separately, since a good candidate may terminate faster than a poor incumbent or cause the actor to inspect more evidence. The same actor-count cap gives a useful systems invariant for batching, but neither assumes equal duration nor predicts cost on a task mix.

    The pairing matters in both directions. A difficult task that defeats both models can contribute a difference close to zero even if its two outcomes have volatile individual rewards. A task where a helpful edit unlocks the solve can contribute a large positive difference. Resampling all individual attempts as if independent would miss both the dependency within a task and the fact that the two checkpoints saw the same task suite. Training-sample variability is at a higher level. With multiple training seeds, the appropriate comparison would first pair within task and training seed when the design supports it, then examine variability across seed-level effects. On binary tasks, counts of tasks solved only by one model are also informative, especially when the absolute evaluation set is as small as AppWorld’s.

    Appendix F.1 reports average update FLOPs per step of 1.14, 1.01, 1.17, and 1.21 times GRPO for the four benchmarks in table order. Matching cumulative update FLOPs thus selects checkpoints that can differ in optimizer steps and exposure. Over-generation, variable branch admission, extra role prompts, environment resets, and long-lived rollout requests remain relevant for end-to-end cost. A MiniSWEBench episode can time out after two hours in training, making the contribution of a slow candidate potentially much larger than the cost of its short curator call. The paper does not provide a cost conversion for that tail.

    7. Adaptation, repeated sampling, and the missing counterfactual

    For a binary verifier, write hkh_khk​ for the conditional probability of success on visit kkk given no previous success. Chain the conditional failure probabilities to obtain

    Pr⁡(solved by K)=1−∏k=1K(1−hk).\Pr(\text{solved by }K)=1-\prod_{k=1}^{K}(1-h_k).Pr(solved by K)=1−k=1∏K​(1−hk​).

    With an independent policy and success probability ppp, all hazards are ppp. For a dependent playbook sequence, each hazard integrates over the memories seen on still-unsolved tasks. Best reward is mechanically nondecreasing as more attempts are included. A dependence diagnostic therefore needs a matched empty or frozen playbook run. In the paper, pass@K for graded tasks is the expected best reward of subsets of independent draws. RSI@K takes the realized best reward among the first KKK dependent visits. Binary hazard interpretation applies directly to AppWorld and the held-out MiniSWEBench evaluation, while partial-credit tasks can be analyzed at a given reward threshold.

    8. Protocol details for an independent evaluation

    The training and evaluation distributions should be written down before collecting any numbers. At collection, record each task ID, encounter number, incumbent and candidate identity, source seed and reflection, group admission, realized external reward, and whether a candidate became the stored incumbent. Store the role messages and generated-token masks alongside these transitions. The classifier for usable reflections and the parser for admissible edits affect how many updates each role sees. Logging counts by benchmark makes it possible to distinguish low adaptation from the absence of a learning signal after filtering.

    A cost ledger should record attempted and completed environment episodes, actual prompt and generation tokens by role, actor and critic update FLOPs, and end-to-end elapsed times. Record reset, timeout, and failed evaluation costs. The paper’s generic test temperature of 0.7 has a MiniSWEBench evaluation exception of 0.6 in Table 5. The per-task reset before visits 3 and 6 is a training rule, while the eight-visit test store persists. Compare checkpoints by cumulative consumed compute with the matching tolerance reported explicitly, and evaluate all comparison arms on the same initial task states and verifier version.

    Appendix C shows why “same task” by itself does not specify the handoff input. The AppWorld reflector inspects entity scope and asks for a pre-submission check. The MiniSWEBench reflector uses an abbreviated interaction record with patch and grading summaries, and its actor prompt pushes toward a successful edit by a turn budget. The Mastermind reflector sees a structured guess history and aims to preserve facts and deductions without printing a secret answer. The MBPP reflector receives failing assertions. The reported formatter can truncate history to 20k tokens and preserves the final environment reply. The renderer and prompts therefore contribute to which evidence enters the reward attribution path.

    9. Limitations

    The paper tests one training run per compared method and two Qwen model sizes, with the two suites confounded with model size. The evidence does not establish how the method scales when the actor already writes reliable playbooks, when verifier signal is scarce, or when restarting an environment is expensive or impossible. Sharing a model means gains in one prompt distribution can coexist with regressions in another. Task-local storage supports same-instance reuse, and cross-task transfer remains open.

    MBPP shows a verifier-specific vulnerability. The failing assertions shown to the actor are also the scored ones. Appendix E reports an early delayed-joint PPO run that exited the test process before a failing assertion could run. The authors changed the grader to require a marker after the assertion and excluded that run. Most reported MBPP evaluations predate this repair, and the stated spot checks for ACE-RL and PPO inspected trajectories from selected steps for exit calls. Appendix G also notes solutions hardcoding visible assertions. The improvement on the reported verifier can be genuine while giving much weaker evidence of program correctness on hidden tests.

    Appendix D.1 makes the estimator limitations unusually inspectable. Training drops all-invalid groups, normalizes noisy samples, and learns the current seed outcome. Test time applies an edit without the training-time best-of-candidates gate. The first AppWorld handoff example and the qualitative examples in Appendix G show plausible grounded behaviors. They identify mechanisms to investigate rather than the causal contribution of each individual tip.

    10. Critical analysis

    Separate the writer from its reader

    The discard control establishes the value of letting the trained system retain edits under its given reader. It does not locate the gain in the reflector, curator, or reader individually. To locate it, form a crossed evaluation with two writers and two readers. Generate each playbook from an identical frozen bank of seeds. Use the same reflector-curator pair from ACE-RL or actor-only GRPO as the writer and evaluate both writers’ outputs under both actor checkpoints. Include empty memory and length-matched unrelated memory under each reader. The latter tests whether changes are trace-specific or act as generic priming. Hold environment seeds fixed where feasible, run several independent actor continuations per playbook, and analyze differences paired by task before aggregating over separately trained checkpoints.

    Let Vw,aV_{w,a}Vw,a​ be the mean reward when writer www produces the playbook for actor aaa. The crossed interaction

    (VACE,ACE−VGRPO,ACE)−(VACE,GRPO−VGRPO,GRPO)(V_{\mathrm{ACE},\mathrm{ACE}}-V_{\mathrm{GRPO},\mathrm{ACE}}) -(V_{\mathrm{ACE},\mathrm{GRPO}}-V_{\mathrm{GRPO},\mathrm{GRPO}})(VACE,ACE​−VGRPO,ACE​)−(VACE,GRPO​−VGRPO,GRPO​)

    tests whether the writer improvement depends on the reader. The same experiment can swap just the curator while holding the reflection fixed. Report both the effect among admitted edits and an intention-to-treat result that scores the incumbent when an output is inadmissible. Those two estimands answer different questions because training may improve syntax or admission separately from the quality of accepted notes.

    Measure credit variance without changing the actor examples

    Collect a shared budget of fully scored handoff branches on a modest subset of tasks. Compute same-input and across-seed advantages from the same leaves, retaining identical actor examples, role prompts, loss masks, and collection checkpoint. Measure the per-role variance of raw score estimates before scaling and the rate of sign reversals after standardization. Randomize which reflection receives the larger edit budget. Compare fixed-denominator and valid-only mean credit and log the admission frequency with outcome by role. This isolates the mechanism in Appendix B.2 and exposes an asymmetry that raw means can conceal.

    A downstream training ablation should then equalize environment episodes and total generation as well as update FLOPs. Comparing two samplers at a single compute budget is informative but can miss a setting in which many cheap descendants beat a few expensive episodes. A frontier coding agent and a small Mastermind agent are likely to occupy different points on that frontier. The data needed to tell them apart are measured costs and paired outcomes.

    Put the no-op back in the experimental choice set

    The selection rule has a one-draw maximum and rewards changing the playbook, even though the safest response to redundant or contradictory evidence may be to retain the old text. Evaluate a real no-op alongside edits on the same environment seeds. Use additional paired retries only when the value gap is within a preset uncertainty band. At test time, cap playbook age or include an explicit rollback when the failing evidence conflicts with its premise. Compare the conditional hazard of first success after each visit, the rate of harmful edits on previously unsolved tasks, and the cost paid per successful task. A stale tip may lose to an empty restart on Mastermind while a precise repository path remains useful on MiniSWEBench. The experiment should preserve that distinction by reporting per-domain error profiles.

    A hidden-verifier variant of MBPP would further separate storing test literals from learning to write useful guidance. Train using the same visible failures, evaluate both visible and hidden assertions on the same candidate program, and preserve the sandbox and timeout across arms. For AppWorld, vary harmless entity identifiers across a transfer subset to test whether tips preserve rules or just names. For software tasks, measure the value and rate of edits that report genuinely new diagnostics, including those that followed a failed seed. Each is a controlled follow-up to the reported evidence, rather than a result from this review.

    Make wall-clock credit assignment visible

    The seed, reflector, and edit paths add serial dependencies before an evaluator can launch. Parallel GPUs can overlap different tasks, but work at one task’s evaluator leaves waits on completion of earlier calls. For one always-admitted branch, the critical path includes one seed, one reflector, one curator, and one evaluator. For a seed pool that is fully synchronized before selection, it also includes the slowest seed; the detached actor-only update can instead consume all seed rewards directly. At fixed compute this creates a different latency distribution even when average GPU utilization appears similar.

    A measured comparison would group tasks by environment latency, then log the time from collection-policy snapshot to first and last admitted evaluation. The difference between those times is useful for quantifying update starvation or policy-age variation. Slow tasks can become underrepresented if collectors stop at a timeout and keep only faster admitted branches. Track reject reason and episode time together for this reason. A policy trained with playbooks that mostly survived a fast environment may lose its edge when new tasks require longer exploration. No runtime conclusions follow from update FLOPs alone.

    For deployment, most additional work arises after an actor that failed could simply retry independently. A low-variance decision experiment can compare adaptive handoff only on unsolved tasks with an independent retry under the same invocation cost.     Hold the environment state fixed as well as the actor

    The reset assumption is part of what makes an ACE-RL handoff evaluable. A candidate and the incumbent can be tested on the same initial world state. An agent continuing in a partially modified production environment faces a different query. If a seed action has already created a record, a memo telling the next actor to begin by creating it can be an effective reminder from a clean start and a harmful instruction from the modified state. With e0e_0e0​ the initial state, e1e_1e1​ the seed’s terminal state, and V(e,P)V(e,P)V(e,P) the value of a memo under a state, the two relevant differences are

    Δreset=V(e0,P′)−V(e0,P),Δcontinue=V(e1,P′)−V(e1,P).\Delta_{\mathrm{reset}}=V(e_0,P')-V(e_0,P),\qquad \Delta_{\mathrm{continue}}=V(e_1,P')-V(e_1,P).Δreset​=V(e0​,P′)−V(e0​,P),Δcontinue​=V(e1​,P′)−V(e1​,P).

    The signs need not agree. Continuing from one seed also changes how credit ought to be shared between the physical effect of the seed’s actions and the text handed onward. For a snapshot-capable benchmark, fork the seed’s terminal state for a within-state comparison, and separately fork the clean initial state. Run the same two playbooks in each fork, preserving verifier rules and actor weights. Record wrong-stage actions, repeated state changes, and the time to complete remaining work. This isolates the value of a handoff in each initial condition. Applying the result to an irreversible service depends on an appropriate sandbox or replay model.

    A typed playbook could separate observations, inferred constraints, and actions to verify. Compare typed tips, free-form tips, and raw evidence at a fixed token budget. Measure both the information the actor can use and what the schema excludes. An easier format may raise admission without raising reward.

    Stratify those trials by seed outcome. Evaluate whether a failed seed’s evidence helps recovery, and whether an edit after success introduces regression. Best-so-far scoring retains an earlier success even if all later attempts fail. Report post-success rewards and time to first solve alongside the headline measure.

    11. Conclusion

    ACE-RL turns the usefulness of an editable, model-written artifact into a trainable verifier signal. Sparse branching gives each writing role comparisons under shared parents while reusing costly candidate evaluations as actor experience. The practical estimator conditions on available evidence, trains the seed actor with current reward, and changes weighting through admission, normalization, and clipping. The exact four-call gradient is a reference for these choices. The matched harness and edit-discarded controls show why training the handoff is worth studying. The next empirical target is the benefit of each learned role under an all-in cost budget and a verifier that tests general behavior beyond the evidence shown to the actor.
